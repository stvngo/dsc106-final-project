Fire After Dark

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires, watched every 5 minutes from 35,786 km above the Pacific.

At 10:31 a.m. on January 7, 2025, the GOES-18 weather satellite flagged a hot pixel in the Santa Monica Mountains, one minute after the Palisades Fire was first reported. About twelve hours later that fire was giving off 25 gigawatts of heat. It was the middle of the night.

Steven Ngo, Aditya Mittal, Leo Wong

Where the satellite saw fire

Each tile is one GOES-18 pixel that was flagged as fire at least twice within two hours, drawn at its true size on the ground: about 2.3 by 2.8 km. Black outlines are the official final perimeters.

How soon did it see each fire?

Grey bars span the reported start times, which disagree between sources. Dots mark the first 5-minute scan that caught each fire. Most fires were caught within a quarter of an hour.

3

When do fires burn hardest?

Wildfires usually follow the sun. Before you look: click the hour of the day when you think a wildfire gives off the most heat.

In the summer 2026 western fire season, fires peaked at 3 p.m. and only 22% of their power came out between 7 p.m. and 5 a.m. During the Santa Ana winds, Palisades and Eaton peaked at 11 p.m. Those first 48 hours held 73% of their energy. When the winds eased, the usual daytime cycle came back.

4

The first 48 hours

Palisades and Eaton both burned hardest after dark, then dropped sharply before dawn on January 8. A second satellite, GOES-16, watching from the other side of the country, saw the same peak and the same drop.

Hover for exact values. Switch to the satellite check to compare GOES-18 with GOES-16.

Most of the energy came out on day one

Each line starts at the fire's reported start. Switch to the share view to see how much of each fire's total energy was already released, hour by hour.

How much energy each fire released

Palisades gave off more radiated energy than every other fire combined, and about twice as much per acre as Eaton.

Takeaway (draft)

For a wind-driven fire, the first hours decide most of what it will do. In Los Angeles those hours came in the middle of the night, the opposite of the usual wildfire cycle, and a satellite saw them unfold in near real time.

Prototype notes

What we have done so far

Draft for the team to rewrite in your own words before submitting. We chose the January 2025 Los Angeles fires as a case study of wildfire spread and pulled every 5-minute GOES-18 fire scan over Los Angeles for January 7 to 31, 2025, along with GOES-16 for the first two days as an independent check. We matched each fire pixel to an official NIFC perimeter and built seven static figures, then rebuilt them here as linked D3 charts in story order. The page already has its main interactions: a guess-then-reveal chart, a satellite toggle, and a replay of each fire's growth with a play button and slider. The text is still placeholder-level, and the charts do not yet respond to scrolling.

What will be most challenging

Draft for the team to rewrite in your own words. The hardest part will be making a 2–3 km satellite pixel feel meaningful next to a city: Palisades covers only about 36 pixels, so the maps can look sparse. Explaining fire radiative power to readers who have never heard of it is the second challenge, and we plan to compare gigawatts with familiar things such as power plants. We also need to describe the pre-dawn drop on January 8 honestly, because both satellites use the same algorithm and the data cannot say what caused it. Finally, turning the page into a scroll-driven story while keeping the animation smooth on a laptop will take careful design and testing.

Data and method

  • GOES-18 Fire/Hot Spot Characterization (ABI-L2-FDCC, AWS bucket noaa-goes18): fire mask and fire radiative power for every 5-minute scan.
  • GOES-16 (noaa-goes16), first 48 hours only, as an independent check.
  • NIFC WFIGS final perimeters, origins and discovery times. Lidia and Sunset have no perimeter and are matched by distance and time.
  • Summer 2026 western US fire data from our Project 3, for the usual daily cycle.
  • Natural Earth coastline. Times are Pacific Standard Time.

Power is a rate, so every value is averaged over the scans in its time step, never summed. Pixels flagged as cloud or smoke ( here) get the average power of nearby pixels. Full processing steps: visualizations/static/README.md.
